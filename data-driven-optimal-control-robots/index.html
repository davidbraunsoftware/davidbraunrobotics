Research

Real-time optimal control of robots

Optimal control computes the best possible motion for a robot, but the calculation relies on a model, and no model is exact. The motion that is optimal in simulation is therefore no longer optimal on the real machine: this is the sim-to-real gap. This research develops optimal control by experiment, in which the robot improves the motion during real experiments, so that measured data, not model predictions, decide how the robot moves.

The sim-to-real gap

Model-based optimal control computes efficiently, but because the model is uncertain, the resulting performance is conservative. Data-driven optimal control reduces the effect of an uncertain model, but needs a large amount of training data, which is slow to collect on real robots. Neither approach alone closes the gap for complex engineering systems, such as assistive, industrial, and medical robots, where accurate models are hard to obtain.

Half model-based, half data-driven

The method developed here combines both approaches. An approximate model speeds up the computation, while measured data decide each improvement, which avoids the errors of predicting future motion with an inexact model. The result is optimal control that is half model-based and half data-driven, and fast enough for real-time hardware-in-the-loop control. Experiments on a three-link, direct-drive, torque-controlled robot demonstrate the benefits of the method.

Optimal control of compliantly actuated robots

Compliant actuators, inspired by muscles and tendons, give robots mechanical adaptability and agility. They also make robots harder to control: the actuators are nonlinear, have limits on range, speed, and force, and offer more ways to move than the task requires. This research shows how impedance control strategies follow from the first principles of optimality, and how to use them on robots with variable impedance actuators. On the DLR Hand Arm System, a humanoid arm now called David, optimal control produced a dynamic ball throw that requires precise timing. The motion emerged from numerical optimization alone, without human data or demonstrations, and the transfer from simulation to the real robot took two weeks.
